I gained insight after figuring something out when tackling problems about the toric code model: Almost every non-trivial effect arises from the boundary.

Here is a list of examples: 
* Boundary conditions of differential equations  
  * vibrational modes
  * fluid dynamics
  * scattering theory
  * distribution of fields, etc. 
* Reflection and refraction of waves at the boundary
* Interface of coexisting phases
* Surface physics
* Fermi surface
* Edge states of symmetry protected topological phases (SPT)
* Symmetry enriched topological (SET) phases and their ground state degeneracy when the boundary condition are open... 

and many other phenomena. In short, symmetry makes physicists' lives easier, but all of a sudden, the boundaries ruthlessly destroy them. One of the mathematical insights is that the topology highly depends on the boundary condition of a system, and it is apparent that two states are radically different if they are in systems with different topologies. Another independent reason is that the symmetry is violated at the boundary, so those physical properties protected by the symmetry disappear there.

In my memory, the first time I realized the fearfulness of boundaries was when I learned the chemical structure of diamond. Right after I learned the chemical bond theory of carbon atoms and the reason why the diamond structure is so stable due to the symmetry, I wondered what would happen at the boundary of a diamond, where the octet rule is apparently violated. Although now I have learned a lot of condensed matter physics and got part of the solutions, I still feel weird.

# The Toric Code Model on a Cylinder

The ground state fo the toric code model on a torus is fourfold degenerate, which is generated by two commuting topological operators, that can be either the Wilson loops or the 't Hooft loops depending on the directions how they wrap around the torus. One of the possible choices is a Wilson loop with a 't Hooft loop in the same direction. These operators generate the 1-form symmetries, and thus the ground states are labeled by their eigenvalues. The number \\(2^2 = 4\\) of possible combinations of the quantum numbers of these operators is exactly the degree of ground state degeneracy. 

Now, let's think about another boundary condition: a cylinder. In this case, we have two open boundaries at the two opposite ends. For the toric code model, there are two types of boundaries, one is called *rough*, where the links stick out and form dangling bonds, while the other is called *smooth*, where there is no dangling bonds. In the bulk, the star operators and the plaquette operators can be still defined in a usual way. However, at the smooth boundary, the plaquette operators can be normally defined but the star operators can only consist of three links, while at the rough boundary, the star operators and be normally defined but the plaquette operators can only consist of three links. Therefore, at the smooth boundary, the product of all star operators becomes an 't Hooft loop

$$
\prod_{s\in \mathrm{s.b.}} S_s = \prod_{i\in \bar{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathrm{s.b.}}}\sigma^x_i = \tilde{T}_{\bar{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathrm{s.b.}}}
$$

Similarly, the product of all plaquette operators along a rough boundary ends up with an Wilson loop

$$
\prod_{p\in \mathrm{r.b.}} P_p = \prod_{i\in \mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{r.b.}}}\sigma^z_i = \tilde{W}_{\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{r.b.}}}
$$

These observations demonstrate that, unlike the to the torus geometry, the 1-form symmetry generators of the toric code model on a cylinder can be constructed or removed by local operators, making them not topologically protected anymore.

For a cylinder, the boundary condition is either (i) both sides are rough, (ii) both sides are smooth, or (iii) one side is rough and the other is smooth. For the first case, the Wilson loop is not topologically protected and can be destroyed by local operators, so the topological ground state degeneracy is only of degree two, which is generated only by the 't Hooft loop. In other words, the density matrix of the ground state is

$$
\rho = \left(\frac{1+\tilde{T}}{2}\right) \prod_{p}\left(\frac{1+P_p}{2}\right) \prod_{s}\left(\frac{1+S_s}{2}\right)
$$

In fact, a most general 

$$
\rho = \left(\frac{1+\tilde{W}}{2}\right) \left(\frac{1+\tilde{T}}{2}\right) \prod_{p}\left(\frac{1+P_p}{2}\right) \prod_{s}\left(\frac{1+S_s}{2}\right)
$$

$$
\prod_{s}\left(\frac{1+S_s}{2}\right) = \left(\prod_{s'\in\mathrm{s.b.}}S_{s'}\right) \prod_{s}\left(\frac{1+S_s}{2}\right) = \tilde{W}\prod_{s}\left(\frac{1+S_s}{2}\right)
$$

therefore, if the cylinder has at least one smooth boundary, the Wilson loop projector can be absorped into the star part

$$
\left(\frac{1+\tilde{W}}{2}\right)\prod_{s}\left(\frac{1+S_s}{2}\right) = \prod_{s}\left(\frac{1+S_s}{2}\right)
$$

causing no net effect.
